# Memo: May 28 Recap

## Why Citation Network and Evolution Analysis Matters

One question that seems important to clarify is: **what is a healthy citation system supposed to do?**

Citation is not just a count. It is not only credit, and not only memory. It may be better understood as a routing system for scientific attention. Citations help decide which papers are seen, reused, trusted, and carried forward. If LLMs become part of how scholars search, summarize, write, review, and justify prior work, then LLMs may become intermediaries in that routing system. This is why the topic may matter beyond citation counts alone.

It is also important to be realistic. Citation is a crucial part of academic evaluation. It affects funding, hiring, promotion, journal reputation, institutional ranking, and perceptions of intellectual contribution. Because citation counts are tied to rewards, the system may also be vulnerable to strategic behavior, such as citation padding, self-citation, citation cartels, coercive citation, and potentially new forms of LLM-assisted citation hacking.

## Possible Field or Arena

This topic seems to sit at the intersection of several arenas:

- Science of science
- Scholarly communication
- Responsible research metrics
- AI and society
- Information systems
- Management and organization studies
- Computational social science
- Research evaluation and academic labor

At a broader level, the topic may be connected to the question: **how does AI affect the way we evaluate human intellectual work?**

Since citation count is still a major part of academic evaluation, any systematic change in citation behavior could potentially change how intellectual value is recognized and rewarded.

## Core Hypothesis Direction

One possible hypothesis to consider is:

> LLMs may help mitigate the Matthew Effect and enhance the long-tail visibility of research, thereby making the citation system more democratic.

In other words, if LLMs help scholars discover less-famous but highly relevant work, they could improve the fairness and diversity of scientific attention. They might make the citation system somewhat less dependent on existing prestige and more open to useful contributions from the long tail.

However, this should not be assumed. LLMs may also amplify the Matthew Effect by repeatedly recommending already famous papers, highly cited authors, and dominant journals. The empirical question may be whether LLMs shift citation behavior toward concentration or toward relevant long-tail discovery.

## Key Conceptual Challenge

One key conceptual challenge is:

> Is highly cited work equal to good work?

The answer is likely no, or at least not always. Citation count captures visibility and influence, but it does not perfectly capture quality. It may be useful to distinguish between **quality dynamics** and **visibility dynamics**.

This suggests that the project should not only count citations. It may also need to consider other dimensions, such as:

- relevance
- correctness
- influence
- originality
- reliability
- usefulness
- field importance
- whether the citation is substantive or merely decorative

Without these dimensions, there is a risk of confusing popularity with quality.

## Refined Research Question

The research question could therefore be framed more broadly:

> How does AI affect citation in academia, not only in terms of citation counts, but in terms of how citation contributes to the health of the scientific system?

The goal may not be simply to ask whether LLMs increase or decrease citations. A deeper question is whether LLMs improve or distort the routing of scientific attention.

A healthy citation system might be expected to route attention toward relevant and reliable work, preserve room for long-tail discovery, remain transparent and auditable, resist gaming, and give valuable contributions a fair chance to be found.

This framing may make the project more than a study of citation bias. It could become a study of how AI may reshape the infrastructure of academic recognition.
