# Citation as an Ecosystem: A Critical Framing

## Core Critique

The research idea is promising, but it has a trap: it can easily become a shallow inequality story. "LLMs amplify the Matthew Effect" is not enough. "LLMs democratize research attention" is also not enough. Both claims are underdefined unless we first answer a harder question: what is a healthy citation system supposed to do?

Citation is not just a count. It is not just credit. It is not just memory. It is a routing system for scientific attention. If LLMs become part of how scholars search, summarize, write, review, and justify prior work, then they become intermediaries in that routing system. That is why this topic matters.

But the normative claim must be sharp. A system where famous papers get many citations is not automatically unhealthy. Some concentration is rational: fields need shared foundations. A system where less-cited papers get more attention is not automatically healthy either. Some long-tail work is obscure because it is irrelevant, wrong, low quality, or outside the focal question. The real question is whether citations flow toward epistemically useful work, or whether they increasingly flow toward whatever is easiest for humans, search engines, and models to retrieve, reproduce, and legitimize.

This is where the papers in the matrix matter. The synthetic LLM studies show a strong high-citation bias: LLMs tend to retrieve or generate highly cited papers (P001, P002, P004, P005). But the real-world evidence is messier. Some work suggests LLM-assisted writing may increase citations or visibility (P003, P015). Other work suggests LLM adopters cite more diverse, younger, less-cited work (P016), while methodological critiques warn that detected-adoption timing can produce spurious effects (P017). So the empirical question should not be "do LLMs create citation bias?" They almost certainly can. The better question is: under what conditions do LLMs make the citation ecosystem more concentrated, more diverse, more accurate, or more fragile?

## 1. Why Research on Paper Citation Is Important

Research on citation is important because citations are one of science's main allocation mechanisms. They allocate attention, legitimacy, memory, and career capital.

First, citations shape discovery. Most researchers cannot read everything. Citation trails tell them where to look, what counts as central, and which claims are connected. A citation network is a map of what a field thinks it knows. If that map becomes distorted, researchers may repeatedly rediscover the same canonical work while missing newer, better, contradictory, or peripheral evidence.

Second, citations shape reputation. Hiring, promotion, funding, awards, journal status, institutional rankings, and field prestige often use citations directly or indirectly. This makes citation behavior consequential beyond knowledge itself. A citation is not just an intellectual gesture; it can become career capital.

Third, citations shape memory. Science forgets as well as remembers. Citation systems decide which contributions remain visible and which sink below the threshold of collective awareness. This matters especially for early-career researchers, non-elite institutions, non-Western authors, interdisciplinary work, negative results, replication studies, and research in less fashionable fields.

Fourth, citations shape the direction of future work. When a few papers become mandatory reference points, they become intellectual infrastructure. That can be good if the papers are foundational and reliable. It can be harmful if their dominance crowds out alternatives, hides uncertainty, or makes a field path-dependent.

Fifth, LLMs may change citation behavior at scale. Before LLMs, citation discovery was mediated by advisors, search engines, databases, journals, and existing reference lists. LLMs add a new layer: they can generate plausible literature maps, suggest citations, summarize fields, and make some references feel more natural than others. The seed papers show that parametric LLMs often internalize existing citation patterns and high-citation bias (P004, P005). If researchers trust these suggestions, LLMs may become "market makers" in scholarly attention.

The importance is therefore not that citations are pure measures of quality. They are not. The importance is that citations are impure measures with real consequences.

## 2. What Citation Means in the Research Ecosystem

In an ecosystem view, citation has several meanings at once.

### Citation as Attention Flow

Citation is a flow of attention from a new paper to prior work. In ecology, energy flows through food webs. In science, attention flows through citation webs. Papers that receive more incoming attention become more visible, more reusable, and more likely to receive future attention. This is the Matthew Effect (P026, P027).

But attention flow is not automatically bad. In an ecosystem, some species are keystone species. In science, some papers are keystone papers. A healthy system does not require a flat distribution of attention. It requires that attention concentration remain justified by relevance, reliability, and usefulness.

### Citation as Credit Assignment

Citation assigns credit, but imperfectly. It says, "this prior work mattered enough to mention." Yet citations can be ceremonial, strategic, coercive, defensive, perfunctory, or socially expected. A citation can reward a genuine source, appease reviewers, signal membership in a community, or borrow legitimacy from a famous name.

This is why a citation study must be careful. If we treat every citation as sincere intellectual debt, we will overclaim. If we treat every citation as empty social signaling, we will miss the real knowledge structure. Citation is both.

### Citation as Legitimacy Signal

Citation also works like a brand signal in markets. A paper cited alongside famous works looks more legitimate. A literature review citing the canonical papers can reassure reviewers that the author knows the field. This is why LLM-generated citations to highly cited papers may feel convincing even when they are generic. Prestige can substitute for specificity.

This is dangerous because LLMs are good at producing legitimate-looking scholarly context. If they over-supply canonical citations, they may increase the appearance of rigor while reducing the precision of intellectual linkage.

### Citation as Search and Navigation Infrastructure

Citation networks help researchers navigate. They are like hyperlinks, roads, or supply chains. A healthy network helps information move from where it is produced to where it is useful. A distorted network creates bottlenecks.

This connects directly to search tools and platforms. Evans's work on electronic publication found narrowing effects in science and scholarship (P028), and Wikipedia experiments show that external knowledge platforms can causally shape scientific attention (P030). LLMs may be another such infrastructure change, but more opaque because they do not merely index; they synthesize and recommend.

### Citation as Reproduction of Social Structure

Citation is also a mechanism through which fields reproduce hierarchy. Elite authors, elite journals, and elite institutions are more visible, easier to remember, and more likely to be included. That does not mean their work is worse. It means visibility and quality are entangled.

This is the hardest part of the project. You cannot simply compare citation counts before and after ChatGPT and call the difference bias. You need to separate at least three things:

- true relevance and quality;
- visibility and accessibility;
- prestige and cumulative advantage.

If the design cannot separate these, the paper will sound important but remain empirically soft.

## 3. Revised Standard of Health for the System

The earlier standard was directionally right, but too internally generated. Academia already has a vocabulary for this problem: responsible metrics, citation context, citation justice, research integrity, open science, and open citation infrastructure.

The revised standard is this:

> A healthy citation ecosystem routes attention and credit toward relevant, reliable, and meaningfully used knowledge while preserving diversity, correctability, transparency, and fair opportunity; it resists prestige lock-in, metric gaming, and unexamined exclusion.

That standard is stricter than "more citations for the long tail." It is also stricter than "cite the most relevant papers." It combines epistemic function, social justice, and governance.

### 1. Claim-Specific Relevance and Meaningful Use

The first standard is not raw relevance to the paper topic. It is relevance to the specific claim being supported. A citation should help readers understand where a claim, method, dataset, contrast, or theoretical move comes from.

This follows the citation-context literature. Citation counts alone hide whether a source is used centrally, perfunctorily, negatively, as background, as a method, or as evidence (P037, P038, P040, P043). Citation context analysis in organizational studies makes the same point: what matters is how ideas are used, tested, critiqued, and transformed, not merely whether a seminal paper appears in the reference list (P039).

For this project, a good dependent variable is not just "citation to high-status paper." It should be closer to "relevance-adjusted, function-aware citation." A citation system is healthier when citations are semantically and rhetorically appropriate, not when they merely optimize coverage or prestige.

### 2. Quality-Aware Attention, Not Citation-as-Quality

A healthy citation system must not confuse citedness with quality. The responsible-metrics literature is blunt on this. DORA warns against using journal metrics as surrogates for article or researcher quality and recommends assessing scientific content directly (P033). The Leiden Manifesto and The Metric Tide similarly emphasize context, transparency, field variation, humility, and reflexivity in the use of metrics (P032, P034). Aksnes, Langfeldt, and Wouters argue that citations capture aspects of scientific impact and relevance, but not all dimensions of research quality, such as plausibility, originality, and societal value (P036).

So the standard should be: citations may indicate attention and use, but a healthy system keeps citation indicators subordinate to judgment about content, rigor, and contribution.

For LLM research, this means the project should not imply that a citation redistribution is good just because it gives low-cited papers more attention. The question is whether LLMs improve the match between contribution and attention.

### 3. Productive Concentration with Escape Routes

Some concentration is healthy. Science needs shared reference points. Business ecosystems need leading firms that scale useful products. Ecological systems often depend on keystone species. The problem is not concentration itself; the problem is blind concentration without escape routes.

The Matthew Effect literature explains how early advantage can become cumulative advantage (P026, P027). The responsible-metrics and open-science literatures suggest the system should avoid letting citation counts, journal prestige, or database visibility become self-fulfilling proxies for worth (P032, P033, P047).

A healthy citation ecosystem has canonical anchors, but it also has channels through which new, corrective, local, interdisciplinary, negative, replication, and non-elite work can enter collective attention.

### 4. Diversity as Epistemic Capacity, Not Cosmetic Balance

The earlier phrase "diversity without noise" needs a sharper academic grounding. Citation justice work argues that skewed citation practices do not only harm individual careers; they can harm the epistemic capacity of the community by limiting whose ideas receive uptake (P041, P042). UNESCO's open-science values also emphasize equity, fairness, diversity, inclusiveness, transparency, and collective benefit (P047).

But diversity cannot mean quota-like citation padding. A healthy system improves the discoverability and uptake of relevant contributions from underrecognized authors, institutions, regions, languages, methods, and fields. It does not add irrelevant citations to create a righteous-looking bibliography.

For this project, "democratization" should be defined as increased visibility for relevant and previously underrecognized work, conditional on topic, claim, method, and quality proxies. Otherwise democratization becomes an aesthetic, not an epistemic improvement.

### 5. Correctability and Temporal Responsiveness

A healthy system must update. It should not keep citing a claim as settled when it has been contradicted, retracted, narrowed, replicated poorly, or superseded. The Hong Kong Principles emphasize rewarding transparent reporting, open science, rigor, and practices that strengthen research integrity (P035). Citation context and polarity work also matters here: a negative, corrective, or contrastive citation is different from uncritical endorsement (P037, P040).

This matters especially for LLMs. Model cutoffs and training redundancy may freeze old citation hierarchies into future writing tools. A healthy LLM-mediated citation system should be sensitive to recency, correction, retraction, and changing evidence status, not merely to historical citation frequency.

### 6. Transparency, Auditability, and Open Citation Infrastructure

Citation health is partly an infrastructure problem. If citation data are closed, inconsistent, or non-machine-readable, the system cannot be audited well. I4OC explicitly frames citations as links that knit knowledge together and calls for structured, separable, open citation data (P045). DORA also recommends making reference lists available and transparent (P033). The Leiden Manifesto and The Metric Tide make transparency and robustness central to responsible metrics (P032, P034).

For LLM-era citation research, this is not a side issue. If commercial databases, closed model training data, and proprietary search systems mediate citation visibility, then the system's attention-routing rules become hard to inspect. A healthy system needs auditable citation data and auditable recommendation mechanisms.

### 7. Low Susceptibility to Gaming and Coercion

Citation is an incentive system, and incentive systems are gamed. The literature on citation manipulation documents self-citation abuse, citation cartels, coercive citation, citation mills, and inappropriate reviewer citation requests (P044, P046). DORA explicitly warns that metrics can be gamed and calls for transparency and anti-manipulation practices (P033).

LLMs may add new forms of gaming: automated citation padding, model-optimized abstracts, citation laundering through AI-generated review sections, and repeated amplification of model-favored canonical works.

A healthy system therefore requires not only better recommendation but better friction: citation verification, motivation, context, and editorial norms that reject scientifically unmotivated citations.

### 8. Reflexive Monitoring of System Effects

The strongest upgrade from responsible-metrics work is reflexivity. The system should monitor the effects of its own measurement and recommendation tools. The Metric Tide's responsible-metrics framework includes reflexivity: recognizing and anticipating systemic effects of indicators (P034). The Leiden Manifesto similarly urges regular scrutiny and updating of indicators (P032).

For this project, the key question is not only whether LLMs change citations once. It is whether LLM-mediated citation practices create feedback loops: models recommend highly cited papers, those papers get cited more, future models see even stronger signals, and the system gradually narrows.

The health standard should therefore include feedback-loop monitoring: concentration, diversity, correction, citation function, manipulation, and exclusion should be tracked over time.

## What This Means for the Research Idea

The idea is important, but it needs a more disciplined normative and empirical core.

A weak version of the paper would say:

> LLMs may amplify the Matthew Effect, which is bad because highly cited papers get more citations.

That is too soft. It assumes concentration is bad, ignores relevance, and does not distinguish synthetic model behavior from real human behavior.

A stronger version would say:

> LLMs are becoming citation intermediaries. We test whether their adoption changes relevance-adjusted attention allocation in science: do they increase dependence on already dominant papers and authors, or do they improve discovery of less-visible but relevant work?

That version is sharper because it makes citations part of an ecosystem, defines the possible harm, and allows the answer to be mixed.

The best version may be even more precise:

> After the public release of ChatGPT, did high-LLM-adoption fields experience a change in the concentration, age, prestige, and semantic relevance of cited references relative to lower-adoption fields?

This can support real empirical work. It also avoids pretending that "Matthew Effect" and "democratization" are moral labels. They are mechanisms. Their health depends on whether they improve or degrade knowledge routing.

## Sharp Risks to the Project

1. **Citation count is not quality.** If the paper treats lower citation concentration as healthier by default, reviewers will attack it.

2. **LLM use is hard to observe.** AI-writing detectors are noisy, culturally biased, and unstable as authors adapt (P012, P017).

3. **Relevance is the missing confounder.** Famous papers may be cited because they are genuinely more relevant. Less-cited papers may be ignored because they are irrelevant. Without semantic relevance controls, the Matthew-effect claim is weak.

4. **Citation lag is severe.** Post-2022 citation effects may reflect publication cycles, preprint behavior, field growth, and indexing delays rather than LLM adoption.

5. **Synthetic behavior may not equal human behavior.** P004 and P005 show what LLMs recommend. But human authors may verify, replace, ignore, or diversify those recommendations.

6. **Democratization can be fake.** More citations to less-cited papers is not necessarily better if those citations are superficial, unsupported, or irrelevant.

7. **The causal story is fragile.** The release of ChatGPT is a broad historical shock, not a clean treatment. Fields differ in trends, publication speed, openness to preprints, and AI exposure.

## Bottom Line

Citation research is important because citations are the circulation system of science. They move attention, credit, legitimacy, and memory. LLMs matter because they may become new organs in that circulation system: not just tools that write text, but tools that decide which prior work becomes visible at the moment of writing.

The health of the system should not be judged by equality, volume, or novelty alone. A healthy citation ecosystem routes attention toward relevant and reliable work, makes citations meaningful and auditable, preserves enough diversity for discovery, corrects stale beliefs, resists gaming, and gives valuable contributions a fair chance to be found.

The research idea is worth pursuing if it studies citation as knowledge infrastructure. It is not worth pursuing if it merely counts whether famous papers get cited more.
